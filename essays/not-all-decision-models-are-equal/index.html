Not All Decision Models Are Created Equal

November 23, 2026 · Updated November 25, 2026 with what triggers the shift

Decision models answer small, typed questions about text, with a probability for every answer. Their best feature is that you can ask several questions about the same text in one request and combine the answers in code. That only works if the questions don't affect each other. On one of the two decision models I tested, they do.

I build phone menus for a living. The menu says "tell me what you're calling about", the caller says something, and the system has to decide where they go. Decision models are a very good fit for that: the menu becomes a choice question ("which of these is the caller asking about?"), and the model returns a probability for every option, plus "none of these". Code takes the top answer if it clears a threshold. Otherwise it asks again.

The design I wanted for one menu was two questions about the same words, asked together: "which topic?" and a separate yes or no, "does the caller want a person?". Code combines them: a named topic wins, and the person route is for callers who give us nothing else. Each question is small and atomic, and the code makes the decision. That's the way these models are meant to be used.

It didn't work on Microsoft's Decision-1.1. The topic answers got worse whenever the person question was in the request. So I took the menu out of the picture and built the smallest test I could.

The test

One choice question, "Which department is the customer asking for?", with six answers: billing, orders, shipping, returns, tech support and none. Ten short customer messages, from clear ("where's my package") to vague ("the thing I ordered"). Each message is sent three ways:

  • alone: just the department question;
  • + English: the department question plus an unrelated yes/no question, "Is the text written in English?";
  • + person: the department question plus "Does the customer ask to speak to a person?".

I ran it on Microsoft-Decision-1.1 on Azure and on TypeSafe's Jev 1.14. Both take the same request format: a state (the text) and a set of named questions. Every shape ran at least twice on Decision-1 and three times on Jev, so the run-to-run variation could be measured. The script and every raw response are linked below.

If the questions are independent, the department answer should come out the same in all three shapes. A yes/no about the language of the text has nothing to do with which department someone wants.

Three things stand out about Decision-1.1.

The answer depends on what else is in the request. "The thing I ordered" is "none" when asked alone and "orders" when anything else rides along. The same question about the same words gets a different top answer.

It doesn't matter what the second question is. The unrelated English question and the related person question shift the department answer by exactly the same amount, to two decimal places, on every message. So it isn't the meaning of the other question. Its presence is enough.

It's perfectly repeatable. Decision-1.1 gave identical numbers on every repeat. This isn't noise; it's the model.

The same question about the same words should get the same answer, whatever else you ask in the request.

Jev behaves differently. Its answers vary slightly from run to run, by up to 0.08 on the vaguest message, because TypeSafe aims for consistency, not determinism: the same input gives similar answers, not identical ones. But adding a second question doesn't move them. Across the three shapes the average shift was 0.02 at most, well inside that run-to-run range, and it never changed which answer came out on top.

Follow-up: a switch, not a sum

Added November 25. Two days later I ran more variations on Decision-1.1 to pin down what triggers the shift: more questions, the questions in the other order, other question types, the same question twice, and longer requests. About 190 more requests, all with the same synthetic messages.

One question or more than one is what matters. With two, three or five questions, the department answer is identical to every decimal place, as long as the request is about the same length. The second question's position doesn't matter: put it first and the department answer is the same. Nor does its type: a yes/no, a choice or a score question all shift the department answer by exactly the same amount. The same department question sent twice under two names gets identical answers both times.

There is also a smaller length step, in short requests only. With two questions, the department answer changes once more between 188 and 189 input tokens. With three questions the step is at 158 to 159, as if each question carried about 30 tokens the count doesn't show. Above the step the answer was identical at every length I tried, up to 2,385 tokens, so this isn't a context limit or truncation. On "cancel it":

The same holds on my real menus. Those requests are 1,000 to 1,800 tokens, well past the length step. With two, three or four questions, the menu answer was identical every time, on all ten phrases I tried, and different from the menu question asked alone. On one menu every phrase lost confidence, one from 0.98 to 0.56. On the other, some gained: one rose from 0.70 to 0.87. So it isn't simply that extra questions make answers worse. Decision-1.1 seems to score a request one way when it holds one question and another way when it holds more. Each way is perfectly repeatable, and the two can disagree by a lot.

That's a useful result for anyone designing around it. With several questions, past the length step, the answers don't move: not with more questions, other questions or longer ones. A single question I couldn't test the same way, because there's no way to make one question longer without changing what it asks. So tune your thresholds with the request shape you'll ship. What you can't do is tune them with one question and ship with two.

Why it matters

With six departments the Decision-1.1 shifts are noticeable. On my real menu, about 40 options, they decided the outcome: one phrase fell from 0.75 alone to 0.38 with a second question attached, from a confident match to "ask the caller again". In a broader set of 26 menu phrases, the average confidence in the right answer fell from 0.96 to 0.89, and three phrases dropped below the 0.7 threshold.

Asking several small questions in one request is the whole reason to use a decision model. You get atomic judgments, each with its own threshold, and code that composes them. If a question's answer depends on its neighbours, you can't tune a threshold once and trust it: adding a question to a request quietly changes the others. The workaround is to send each question as its own request, which multiplies the calls against your rate limits and gives up the main reason for using a decision model.

My team is reporting it to Microsoft with the full reproduction. In the meantime, the menu ships with a single choice question and no side questions. That works well enough. The two-question design, which tested best for routing callers who want a person, waits until a second question stops changing the first.

The takeaway

"Decision model" describes an interface: questions in, a probability for every answer out. It doesn't promise behaviour. Two models can take the same request format and still disagree on something as basic as whether your questions are independent. If your design depends on that, and with decision models it usually does, test it: the same question alone and with a second question attached, a few times each. It costs a few dozen requests, and it would have saved me a day.

Data and reproduction

  • repro.py. The test. It sends the same department question in the three shapes to either model, and prints the request count without sending until you add --send. Jev reads TYPESAFE_API_KEY. Decision-1 uses an Azure CLI login, plus D1_ENDPOINT and D1_DEPLOYMENT for your own deployment.
  • results-d1.json, repeat-d1.json. Microsoft-Decision-1.1 (version 1, DataZoneStandard), every request and its department probabilities, first run and repeat.
  • results-jev.json, repeat-jev.json. Jev 1.14.0 (jev-1.14.0), first run and two repeats.
  • isolate.py, isolate_length.py, isolate_sweep.py. The follow-up on Decision-1.1 (2026-11-25): more questions, the other order, noul and score questions, the same question twice, and a length sweep that pads a neutral second question one word at a time. Same environment as repro.py. Results in isolate-d1.json, isolate-length-d1.json (with token counts) and isolate-sweep-d1.jsonl. Every response keeps its request-id headers.
  • Method notes. Each model was tested on 2026-11-23 in one session. "Shift" is the change in the probability of the answer the model chose for the message alone, averaged over repeats. Requests that hit a rate limit were retried, never scored as answers.
